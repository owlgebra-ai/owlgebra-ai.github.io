o-means: 100x faster k‑means in a quarter of e-means’ time

Nov 12, 2026

Rahul Bajaj, Jaya Nupur, Anuj Garg

Code Paper (PDF)

o-means is a GPU k-means for large k: millions of vectors and tens of thousands of centroids, the job you run to build an IVF index or a codebook. It reaches the same clustering error as e-means in about a quarter of the time, and in a fraction of a second where a classic k-means library takes seconds to minutes.

It started with a profile, not with an idea. e-means, which Meta FAIR published earlier this month, had just shown that large-k clustering needs two or three passes over the data where Lloyd’s algorithm needs twenty-five. We ran it on our own card and asked what you ask of any program you want to make faster: where does the time go? The answer was lopsided. Four fifths or more of every fit went into one operation, measuring the distance from each point to every centroid in order to keep the nearest.

Nobody searches like that. Looking for a book, you do not walk past every shelf in the library; you find the aisle and search there. A point in a codebook of 16,384 centroids is nowhere near most of them, and it should not have to visit them to find that out. So o-means gives the centroids aisles. It groups them into cells of about 128, sends each point to its dozen or two nearest cells, and compares it only with the centroids it finds there. That is between 6 and 22 percent of the comparisons e-means makes, depending on k and the dimension.

The catch is that the library keeps rearranging itself. Centroids move after every batch, so the cells are out of date almost as soon as they are drawn, and an approximate search that drifts will quietly train worse centroids. Most of the work in o-means went into keeping that search accurate inside a loop that never holds still. We then went back to the profile for the smaller pieces: kernels written for the card we have, and a better way to finish a run that is only one epoch long.

On eight datasets, from a quarter of a million to ten million vectors, on one RTX 5090:

  • The same error in a quarter of the time. o-means reaches e-means’ error in 14 to 39 percent of e-means’ time, 22 percent in the geometric mean. On GooAQ (1.46 million vectors, 768 dimensions, 16,384 clusters) a two-epoch e-means fit takes 0.50 s and o-means is at the same error after 0.11 s. On SPACEV with 10 million vectors and 65,536 clusters it is 2.23 s against 0.36 s.
  • Lower error in the same time. Stop both at the moment e-means finishes one epoch and o-means’ error is 2.0 to 3.9 percent lower. At two epochs it is 0.7 to 1.3 percent lower. For scale, running o-means to the end of its longest budget improves on 25 Lloyd iterations by 0.73 to 1.65 percent in total.
  • A sliver of the arithmetic of classic k-means. Twenty-five Lloyd iterations cost 25 full passes of distance computations. e-means matches their error in 2 to 3 passes. o-means matches it with the arithmetic of 0.15 to 0.45 of one pass.
  • Hundredths of a second instead of seconds. Flash-KMeans and faiss, the two GPU k-means libraries we timed, take between 1.35 and 123 seconds for 25 iterations on these datasets. o-means reaches the same error in 0.020 to 0.37 seconds.
  • Nothing new to tune. o-means takes the same arguments as e-means and has the same single knob, the number of epochs. Peak GPU memory in the test runs was 7.5 GB, data included.
  • Open source, inside faiss. We have contributed o-means to our fork of faiss as a contrib package, next to e-means and under the same MIT licence, so anyone already using faiss can try it by changing one import.

Two things to keep in mind while reading. Every timing here comes from one RTX 5090, where e-means runs its portable Triton kernels and not the H100 kernels it was tuned on; about 1.4× of our gain is faster kernels for this card and about 3× is the algorithm. And the search inside o-means is approximate, though every error we report is measured exactly, on rows the fit never saw. The limitations are listed near the end.

01

Where large k shows up

Tens of thousands of centroids sounds like a niche. It is the normal case in several places where embeddings are stored, searched or cleaned at scale.

  • Vector search, and the retrieval behind RAG. An IVF index starts by clustering the corpus, and at query time only the nearest clusters are scanned. The faiss guidelines ask for 4√n to 16√n clusters below a million vectors, 65,536 for one to ten million, 262,144 for ten to a hundred million and about a million clusters beyond that. Every time the embedding model changes, the corpus is re-embedded and this clustering is run again.
  • Late-interaction retrieval. Systems in the ColBERT family store one vector per token, not per document, and compress them against a set of centroids whose number grows with the square root of the number of token vectors. ColBERTv2 clusters roughly 600 million MS MARCO token embeddings into 218 = 262,144 centroids.
  • Deduplicating and curating training data. Finding near-duplicates among hundreds of millions of images or documents by comparing every pair is out of reach. Pipelines such as SemDeDup first cluster the embeddings into tens of thousands of groups and compare only within a group.
  • Visual vocabularies. Image retrieval and place recognition quantise local descriptors into “visual words”. Philbin et al. built a vocabulary of one million words from 16.7 million descriptors, and found that retrieval quality peaked there.
  • Codebooks. Vector quantisers for compression, and the tokenizers that turn images or audio into discrete codes, need codebooks of thousands to tens of thousands of entries, and k-means is the usual way to build or initialise them.

These cases have one thing in common: k grows with the data, often like √n. One pass of exhaustive assignment costs n·k distances, so the bill grows like n1.5. Ten times more data means about thirty times more work per pass, which is why the cost per pass is worth attacking.

02

Where k-means spends its time at large k

k-means looks for k centroids that minimise the mean squared distance from each of n points to its nearest centroid. Lloyd’s algorithm alternates two steps: assign every point to its nearest centroid, then move every centroid to the mean of its points. The assignment is the expensive half. One pass costs n·k distance computations in d dimensions, and at k = 16,384 or 65,536 nothing else in the loop comes close. We profiled e-means on our card: 80 to 86 percent of its wall time is the kernel that computes distances and takes the minimum.

There are two ways to make that cheaper. You can make fewer passes, or you can do less work per pass. e-means did the first. o-means keeps that and adds the second.

03

What e-means does

e-means is mini-batch k-means with the update rule of a VQ-VAE codebook. Each centroid is the ratio of two exponential moving averages, a running sum of the points assigned to it and a running count. After each batch both averages move a step toward the batch’s statistics, and the step size follows a cosine schedule from large to small. Clusters that stop receiving points are revived by splitting a busy centroid in two. Batch size and peak step are not tuned by the user; they come from two power laws in n, k and the number of epochs T that the authors fitted once. T is the only knob. At the end the centroids are replaced by the plain means of the last epoch’s points, under the labels those points received as they went by.

The e-means paper reports that this reaches the error of 25 Lloyd iterations in one to three epochs, which on an H100 is 10 to 45 times sooner than Flash-KMeans. o-means is built on it: the objective, the random starting centroids, the moving-average update, the two laws, the cosine schedule and the revival rule are e-means’, and we did not set out to improve them.

What e-means leaves alone is the cost of an epoch. Every batch is still compared with all k centroids. That is the part o-means rebuilds, and it is less of a swap than it sounds. The search has to stay accurate while the centroids move underneath it. It has to survive a revival rule that relocates thousands of centroids during the first epoch. And on a GPU, where brute force is exactly what the hardware is best at, it has to be laid out so that doing a tenth of the comparisons is actually faster.

04

What o-means changes

We took three pieces out of the profile, the smallest first. Each one changed what the next measurement looked like, which is most of the reason for doing them in order.

First, kernels that fit the card

e-means’ fastest kernels are written for the H100. On an RTX 5090 it falls back to a portable backend, so the first step had nothing to do with the algorithm: we rewrote the inner loop for the card on the desk. Distances and the minimum are fused into one half-precision kernel, the update into another, and revival no longer waits on the host. We call e-means’ algorithm, untouched, on these kernels v0. It was about one and a half times faster than the reference. More usefully, it gave us a baseline that shares our plumbing: whatever o-means gains over v0 comes from the algorithm.

Second, a better ending for short runs

The second piece came from looking closely at a single one-epoch run. e-means finishes by averaging the last epoch’s points under the labels they received as they went by, labels that are up to an epoch old. When the run is several epochs long that is harmless. When the run is one epoch long, the last epoch is the whole run, and the average includes everything from the chaotic start, when the centroids were still random rows of the data.

So below two epochs o-means weights each batch by its recency, with a time constant of one epoch, and when a centroid is split it hands half of the accumulated sums to the child. At one epoch this lowers the error by 0.34 to 0.70 percent on the five datasets where we measured it. It is not free everywhere. From two epochs up the same trick raises training error (by 0.24 to 0.32 percent at two epochs), so there o-means goes back to e-means’ closing mean.

Third, aisles for the centroids

This is the large piece. Group the k centroids into cells of about 128. There are G = k/128 cells, rounded up to a multiple of 16: 128 cells for k = 16,384 and 512 for k = 65,536. The grouping is itself a small k-means, run on the centroids instead of the data. Then, for each point in a batch:

  1. compute its distance to the G cell centres and keep the p nearest cells;
  2. compute its distance to the centroids inside those p cells, and to no others;
  3. take the minimum.

p is 16 below 512 dimensions, 12 from 512 up, and 24 when k is above 16,384.

The saving is easy to count, and it grows with the codebook, which is why the largest ones gain the most. The measured counts, which include the cost of building the cells, agree with the arithmetic: 13.4 percent of the dense comparisons on DEEP 1M and 6.0 percent on SPACEV 10M for a one-epoch fit.

Fewer comparisons are only faster if the GPU can do them without waiting on memory, and a GPU is happiest multiplying two dense blocks. So we keep the centroid table physically sorted by cell. Each cell is one contiguous block, and a tile of points can be multiplied against it on tensor cores like a small dense problem. A batch takes three kernel launches: one scores the points against all cell centres and drops each (point, cell) pair into that cell’s bucket, one walks the buckets, one takes the minimum over each point’s p partial results. Nothing is read back to the host inside the loop.

The first version was fast, and its error was worse

The cells describe where the centroids were when the cells were built. Our first schedule rebuilt them twice per epoch, which seemed generous. It was not. Between one eighth and one half of the first epoch, 10 to 28 percent of points were being assigned to something other than their nearest centroid, and the error after one epoch rose by 0.53 percent on DEEP 1M and 0.76 percent on arXiv-768.

We assumed the early centroids were simply too crude to group. That was the wrong suspect. The cause was revival: during the first epoch e-means splits 9 to 12 thousand of 16,384 centroids, and a freshly split centroid still sits in its donor’s cell on paper while it drifts away in practice. Points that belonged to the child were looking for it in the wrong aisle.

Two changes fixed it. During the first epoch the cells are refreshed after every revival check, which is every five batches; the refresh reassigns centroids to the existing cell centres and re-sorts the table, with no k-means iterations, so it is cheap. After the first epoch the cells are rebuilt every half epoch. And every split is recorded as a pointer from donor to child, so a point that lands on a donor also checks the donor’s child and grandchild before it decides.

With both in place, 0.5 to 2.4 percent of points missed their nearest centroid on four of the five datasets where we measured it. GloVe-200 was the exception at 4 to 13 percent, which cost it about 0.1 percent in error. After the same number of epochs an o-means fit ends 0.02 to 0.13 percent higher in training error than the same fit with exact assignment, and each epoch takes between a quarter and a half as long (one seed, four datasets).

Measured together on five datasets, the three pieces stack like this. Kernels alone reached e-means’ error 1.51 times sooner. The new ending for short runs took that to 1.66. Two-level assignment took it to 4.44.

05

How we measured

We adopted the evaluation methodology of the e-means paper, so that the two methods are compared like for like, and fixed our own additions in a written protocol that was locked before any run was scored. The conventions that determine what each number means are as follows.

Both methods start from the same k random rows of the data. The data is on the GPU before the clock starts. The clock covers everything the method does: casting, building its state, every pass, the closing step. Each fit gets an untimed warm-up call first, and fits shorter than two seconds are timed three times and the median kept. e-means and o-means fits are interleaved in one process, so drift in the machine hits both. e-means is called the way its own benchmark calls it, in bfloat16 with the batch law the paper prescribes for each size.

Error is the mean squared distance from held-out rows to their nearest centroid, computed with exact assignment. The approximation inside o-means is never used for scoring.

A method that takes an effort knob produces a curve of error against time, so comparing two of them needs a rule. Ours: run e-means at T = 1, 2, 3 and 5 epochs and o-means at ten efforts from 0.8 to 12.8 epochs, average over seeds, and for each T find the earliest time at which o-means’ curve is at or below e-means’ error. Between two neighbouring o-means points we interpolate in log-time, but only if their times differ by at most 1.6×. The speed-up at T is e-means’ time divided by that crossing time. Where we quote a single figure for a suite, it is the geometric mean of these speed-ups over datasets and T = 1, 2, 3. We also report it with measured points only, without interpolation.

The results below are on eight datasets: SPACEV at 1M and 10M, ImageNet-CLIP, and five text and image embedding sets from the VIBE benchmark with 768 to 3,072 dimensions. The acceptance criteria were fixed in advance: a geometric-mean speed-up of at least 1.15, no single dataset and budget below 0.95, and error at e-means’ five-epoch time no more than 0.16 percent worse, which is the noise floor we measured for that comparison. The eight were run once, on the release commit.

For scale, e-means measured against an identical copy of itself through the same harness came out at 1.0001. Against copies of itself with different internal seeds it came out a little below one, 0.95 to 0.99 depending on the budgets tested, so the rule leans slightly against the candidate; two such controls differed from each other by 0.013.

07

Results against Lloyd’s k-means

The e-means paper’s reference for classic k-means on a GPU is Flash-KMeans run for 25 full-batch iterations from the same random start, and we kept it. When the benchmark was built we recorded that run’s error and time for every dataset and seed. Here we ask how long e-means and o-means take to reach the same error. This comparison was not among the pre-registered criteria; we computed it afterwards from runs that already existed.

Count distance computations first, because that number does not depend on anyone’s kernels. Lloyd spends 25 passes’ worth. e-means reaches the same error with a run of 2 to 3 epochs. o-means reaches it at an effort of 1.6 to 3.2 epochs, each of which costs a fraction of a pass, for a total of 0.15 to 0.45 passes’ worth. On all eight datasets that is less arithmetic than half of one Lloyd iteration.

Wall-clock needs more care. On our card Flash-KMeans takes 1.35 to 51.6 seconds for its 25 iterations and o-means reaches the same error in 0.020 to 0.37 seconds. That is 69 to 274 times sooner, geometric mean 126; by the same measure e-means is 27 times sooner than Flash-KMeans. Those multiples come with a caveat. Flash-KMeans’ distance kernel runs at 25 to 103 TFLOPS on the 5090, where we measured about 230 with cuBLAS in half precision, and its 25 iterations take about 4 to 16 times longer here than the e-means paper reports for the same datasets on an H100. Part of the multiple is a baseline that is not at home on this GPU.

Most people who run k-means on a GPU run faiss, so we timed that too: faiss 1.14.3 from conda-forge (the wheel on PyPI is not built for the RTX 5090), its own k-means loop with brute-force single-precision search, 25 iterations from the same start, with the data already on the card. It ends within 0.02 percent of Flash-KMeans’ error, so the two agree on what 25 iterations buy. It is not the faster of the two. faiss takes 3.09 to 123 seconds and is slower than Flash-KMeans on six of the eight datasets; it wins on the two with the widest vectors, AG-News and SimpleWiki. Measured against faiss, o-means reaches the same error 78 to 337 times sooner, geometric mean 181, and a similar caution applies: we ran faiss with its default single-precision search, which does 26 to 52 TFLOPS here, and did not try its half-precision option. Calling faiss.Kmeans on arrays in host memory, the way most code does, takes a further 17 to 58 percent on the seven datasets of up to two million rows where we timed it.

Quality is not where these methods differ much. Run to the end of its ladder, 12.8 epochs, o-means ends 0.73 to 1.65 percent below Lloyd’s error, in 15 to 44 times less time than Flash-KMeans’ 25 iterations. About one percent of MSE is the size of the prize. What changes is how long it takes to collect.

08

Kernels or algorithm

e-means was built and tuned on H100s, where its fastest distance kernels run. They do not run on an RTX 5090, so the reference in this post used e-means’ Triton backend, and some of our speed-up is simply better kernels for this card. To separate the two we measured v0, which is e-means’ algorithm, unchanged, on our kernels. It performs as many distance computations as e-means, or more. Whatever it gains is kernels.

On the eight datasets that is 1.43 from kernels times 3.13 from the algorithm. The split varies by dataset. The algorithm’s share is 1.96 on SimpleWiki and 4.89 on SPACEV 10M: with more centroids, a smaller fraction of them is searched.

If you run e-means on an H100, expect the kernel share to shrink or disappear. We could not test that. The algorithm’s share has some support that does not depend on the card: at the points where o-means matches e-means it has done between 0.06 and 0.30 of e-means’ distance computations, 0.13 in the geometric mean. But look at the exchange rate. About 7 times fewer distance computations bought about 3 times less time. The two-level kernel gathers scattered rows, works on cells of uneven size, and pays for rebuilding the cells, which is a quarter of a one-epoch fit on DEEP 1M. On a GPU whose dense kernel is faster relative to its memory traffic, the rate could be worse. We do not know.

09

Limitations

  • One GPU. All timings are from one RTX 5090, with e-means on its Triton backend. On an H100 the kernel part of the gain should shrink; the algorithmic part is not yet measured there.
  • Approximate search during training. A small share of points, 0.5 to 2.4 percent on typical data, misses its nearest centroid. The errors we report are measured with exact assignment, and assign returns exact labels when you need them.
  • Probe counts chosen on a few datasets. Cosine-like data such as GloVe-200 misses more often (4 to 13 percent) and gives up about 0.1 percent in error; a larger nprobe narrows that, at some cost in time.
  • Smaller gains at small k and on some synthetic shapes. With 8,192 centroids there are only 64 cells to choose from. On synthetic, well-separated mixtures at 1,024 dimensions and above, a few cells can hold most of the centroids and the speed-up fades, with quality unaffected. The speed-up did not fade on any real dataset we ran.
  • Long budgets. We tested up to 12.8 epochs. On four of the eight datasets, e-means at eight epochs ends 0.02 to 0.16 percent lower than anything on that range, at 2.7 to 3.2 times the time.
  • Scale of the evaluation. Three to five seeds per dataset, where the e-means paper uses ten; speed-ups are read from seed-averaged curves with interpolation (4.08 without). GPU only, Euclidean distance, up to 10 million vectors and 65,536 centroids.
10

Using it

o-means is open source. We contributed it to our fork of faiss as a contrib package, next to e-means, under faiss’s MIT licence, with tests and a benchmark script, so that anyone can use it, check our numbers and improve on it. It takes the same arguments as e-means.

import torch
from faiss.contrib.o_means import Omeans

# x: float32 CUDA tensor [n, d], already on the GPU
om = Omeans(d, k, nepochs=2.5)
centroids, labels = om.train(x)        # starts from k random rows
distances, nearest = om.assign(x)      # full search, for when you need labels

nepochs is the number of passes over the data, the same unit as e-means’ epochs, and it can be fractional. In our runs o-means matched 25 Lloyd iterations at between 1.6 and 3.2 epochs, and the ladder we tested runs from 0.8 to 12.8. nlist and nprobe override the number of cells and how many of them each point searches; the defaults are the rule described above. The labels that train returns come from the approximate search, taken while the centroids were still moving, so call assign when the labels matter. Peak GPU memory in our test runs, data included, was 7.5 GB.

The package includes a small script that runs o-means and e-means on one dataset and prints time and error:

python benchs/o_means/bench_o_means.py --nepochs 1 2 3

The paper has the full method and the evaluation protocol.
