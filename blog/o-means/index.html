o-means: index the centroids, don’t scan them

Nov 12, 2026

Rahul Bajaj, Jaya Nupur, Anuj Garg

Code Paper (PDF)

o-means is a GPU k-means algorithm for large k: millions of vectors clustered into tens of thousands of centroids, the job that builds an IVF index or a vector-quantisation codebook. o-means reaches the clustering error of e-means in 22% of e-means’ time. It reaches the error of 25 Lloyd iterations more than 100 times sooner than Flash-KMeans or faiss GPU.

o-means takes the same arguments as e-means and has the same single knob, the number of epochs. It ships as a contrib package in our fork of faiss under the MIT licence.

01

What is new

  1. Two-level IVF assignment over the centroids. The centroids are grouped into cells of about 128. Each point is compared with the cell centres, then only with the centroids in its nearest cells. At equal error, o-means performs about 7 times fewer distance computations than e-means.
  2. Online index maintenance under centroid drift. Centroids shift after every batch, and thousands are split during the first epoch, so a static index goes stale within a few batches. o-means refreshes the index every five batches during the first epoch, rebuilds it every half epoch afterwards, and keeps a pointer from every split centroid to its child. On typical data the share of points that miss their nearest centroid falls from 10–28% to 0.5–2.4%.
  3. Cell-contiguous centroid layout with bucketed dense kernels. The centroid table is stored sorted by cell, so each cell is a contiguous block that runs as a small dense matrix product. A batch takes three kernel launches and never returns to the host.
  4. Exponentially recency-weighted closing mean. For fits shorter than two epochs, the closing average gives recent batches more weight. This lowers the error of a one-epoch fit by 0.34–0.70%.
02

Results at a glance

  • Equal-error speed-up of 4.49× over e-means. In the geometric mean o-means reaches e-means’ error in 22% of e-means’ time, and it is between 2.54× and 7.25× faster on individual datasets and targets.
  • Equal-time error, distance-computation budget and wall-clock time against Lloyd. At the moment e-means finishes one epoch, o-means’ error is 2.0–3.9% lower. To match the error of 25 Lloyd iterations, Lloyd spends 25 passes of distance computations, e-means 2 to 3, and o-means 0.15 to 0.45. In wall-clock time o-means reaches that error in 0.020–0.37 s, where Flash-KMeans and faiss GPU take 1.35–123 s. The geometric-mean factor is 126× against Flash-KMeans and 181× against faiss GPU.
03

Background

Assignment is the cost

k-means looks for k centroids that minimise the mean squared distance from each of n points to its nearest centroid. Lloyd’s algorithm alternates two steps: assign every point to its nearest centroid, then move every centroid to the mean of its points. Assignment is the expensive step. One pass costs n·k distance computations in d dimensions.

Large k is the normal case wherever embeddings are indexed or compressed at scale:

  • IVF indexes for vector search and RAG. The faiss guidelines call for 65,536 clusters at one to ten million vectors.
  • Token codebooks for late-interaction retrieval, as in ColBERTv2.
  • Near-duplicate removal for training data, as in SemDeDup.
  • Codebooks for vector quantisers and discrete tokenizers.

In all of these k grows with the data, often like √n, so the cost of one pass grows like n1.5. Ten times more data means about thirty times more work per pass.

We profiled e-means on an RTX 5090. Between 80% and 86% of its wall time goes to one kernel, the one that computes point-to-centroid distances and takes the minimum.

There are two ways to reduce that cost: make fewer passes, or do less work per pass. e-means does the first. o-means keeps it and adds the second.

e-means in brief

e-means is mini-batch k-means with the update rule of a VQ-VAE codebook.

  • State. Each centroid j keeps a running sum Sj of the points assigned to it and a running count Nj. The centroid is Sj / Nj.
  • Update. After each batch, both move towards the batch’s sum sj and count nj for that centroid: 
    S_j ← (1 − α) · S_j + α · s_j
N_j ← (1 − α) · N_j + α · n_j
  • Schedule. The step size α follows a cosine schedule from large to small.
  • Revival. A centroid that stops receiving points is revived by splitting a busy centroid in two.
  • No tuning. Batch size and peak step size come from two power laws in n, k and the number of epochs T, fitted once by the authors. T is the only knob.
  • Closing step. At the end, each centroid is replaced by the plain mean of the last epoch’s points, under the labels those points received during that epoch.

The e-means paper reports that this reaches the error of 25 Lloyd iterations in at most three epochs, which on an H100 is 10 to 45 times sooner than Flash-KMeans.

o-means keeps e-means’ objective, random-row initialisation, moving-average update, two laws, cosine schedule and revival rule. It changes three things: how points are assigned, how short runs finish, and the kernels.

Relation to earlier approximate k-means

Replacing exact assignment with an approximate nearest-centroid search is an established idea. Approximate k-means (Philbin et al., 2007) searches the centroids with randomised k-d trees, rebuilt once per full pass, and hierarchical k-means (Nistér and Stewénius, 2006) arranges them in a tree. These methods assign a whole pass of data against centroids that hold still.

A mini-batch method does not offer that. The centroids change after every batch, and e-means’ revival rule relocates thousands of them during the first epoch. o-means contributes an approximate search that stays accurate under those conditions, in a layout that is faster than dense search on a GPU, the workload GPUs handle best.

The five subsections below describe each part.

1. Two-level assignment

The cells are an IVF index built over the centroids instead of over the data. Building it is a small k-means run on the k centroids, which produces nlist cell centres and assigns each centroid to one cell.

To assign a point, o-means:

  1. computes its distance to the nlist cell centres and keeps the nprobe nearest cells;
  2. computes its distance to the centroids inside those cells, and to no others;
  3. takes the minimum.

The saving per point follows from the defaults:

kdnlistnprobeComparisons per pointShare of exact
8,192≥ 512641264 + 12 × 128 = 1,60020%
16,384< 51212816128 + 16 × 128 = 2,17613%
16,384≥ 51212812128 + 12 × 128 = 1,66410%
65,536< 51251224512 + 24 × 128 = 3,5845.5%

The saving grows with k, so the largest codebooks gain the most. Measured counts, which include the cost of building the cells, agree with the arithmetic: 13.4% of the exact comparisons on DEEP 1M and 6.0% on SPACEV 10M for a one-epoch fit.

The search is approximate. nprobe sets the trade-off between comparisons and missed nearest centroids.

2. Keeping the cells current

An index over the centroids describes where the centroids were when it was built. In a mini-batch method that description expires quickly, for two reasons:

  • Drift. Every centroid moves after every batch.
  • Revival. During the first epoch e-means splits 9 to 12 thousand of 16,384 centroids. A newly split centroid starts in its donor’s cell and then moves away, while the index still lists it there. Points that belong to the new centroid search the wrong cell.

o-means uses three mechanisms to keep the index valid:

  • Refresh, every five batches during the first epoch. A refresh keeps the cell centres, reassigns every centroid to its nearest cell centre and re-sorts the table. It runs no k-means iterations, so it is cheap. It is scheduled after every revival check.
  • Rebuild, every half epoch after the first epoch. A rebuild recomputes the cell centres with k-means over the centroids.
  • Split pointers. Every split is recorded as a pointer from the donor to its child. A point whose nearest centroid is a donor also checks the donor’s child and grandchild before it decides.

Why this schedule. With rebuilds every half epoch and nothing else, 10–28% of points were assigned to a centroid other than their nearest between one eighth and one half of the first epoch. The error after one epoch rose by 0.53% on DEEP 1M and 0.76% on arXiv-768. Revival was the cause, which is why the refresh follows the revival check.

Accuracy with all three in place. On four of five development datasets, 0.5–2.4% of points miss their nearest centroid. GloVe-200 is the exception at 4–13%, which costs it about 0.1% in error.

Cost of the approximation. After the same number of epochs, a fit with two-level assignment ends 0.02–0.13% higher in training error than the same fit with exact assignment, and each epoch takes between a quarter and half as long (one seed, four datasets).

3. GPU layout

Fewer comparisons are only faster if the GPU can run them as dense matrix products without waiting on memory. o-means therefore keeps the centroid table physically sorted by cell. Each cell is one contiguous block, and a tile of points is multiplied against it on tensor cores like a small dense problem.

A batch takes three kernel launches:

  1. Score the points against all cell centres and place each (point, cell) pair in that cell’s bucket.
  2. Process the buckets: multiply each cell’s block of centroids against the points that search it.
  3. Take the minimum over each point’s nprobe partial results.

Nothing is read back to the host inside the loop.

4. A recency-weighted finish for short runs

e-means finishes by averaging the last epoch’s points under the labels they received during that epoch. Those labels are up to one epoch old. In a run of several epochs this is harmless. In a one-epoch run the last epoch is the whole run, so the average includes the earliest batches, when the centroids were still random rows of the data.

For nepochs below 2, o-means weights each batch by its recency, with a time constant of one epoch. When a centroid is split, half of its accumulated sums go to the child.

At one epoch this lowers the error by 0.34–0.70% on the five development datasets. From two epochs upwards the same weighting raises training error, by 0.24–0.32% at two epochs, so o-means uses e-means’ closing step there.

5. Kernels for the RTX 5090

This part is implementation, not algorithm. e-means’ fastest kernels are written for the H100, and on an RTX 5090 it runs a portable Triton backend. We wrote kernels for the 5090:

  • one fused half-precision kernel computes distances and the minimum;
  • a second applies the update;
  • revival runs on the device without waiting on the host.

We call e-means’ algorithm, unchanged, on these kernels v0. v0 isolates the kernel gain: whatever o-means gains over v0 comes from the algorithm.

05

Results

Setup

  • Hardware. One RTX 5090. The data is on the GPU before timing starts.
  • Evaluation datasets. Eight: SPACEV at 1M and 10M vectors, ImageNet-CLIP, and five text and image embedding sets from the VIBE benchmark with 768 to 3,072 dimensions. Five seeds per dataset, three for SPACEV 10M, 38 runs in total.
  • Development datasets. The component measurements in the algorithm section come from five development datasets, among them DEEP 1M, arXiv-768 and GloVe-200.
  • Error. Mean squared distance from held-out rows to their nearest centroid, computed with exact assignment. o-means’ approximate search is never used for scoring.
  • Timing. The clock covers everything a method does, from casting to the closing step. Both methods start from the same k random rows, and their fits are interleaved in one process. Fits shorter than two seconds are timed three times and the median is kept.
  • Baseline configuration. e-means is called the way its own benchmark calls it: in bfloat16, with the batch law its paper prescribes for each problem size.
  • Equal-error speed-up. e-means runs at T = 1, 2, 3 and 5 epochs, and o-means at ten values of nepochs from 0.8 to 12.8. The speed-up at T is e-means’ time divided by the earliest time at which o-means’ seed-averaged error is at or below e-means’ error, interpolating in log-time between neighbouring measured points. Summary figures are geometric means over datasets and T = 1, 2, 3.
  • Locked protocol. The acceptance criteria were fixed in writing before any run was scored, and the eight datasets were run once, on the release commit. The measured noise floor of the equal-time error comparison at five epochs is 0.16%.

The paper has the full protocol and the control runs.

Against e-means

Time to the same error. o-means needs 14–39% of e-means’ time, 22% in the geometric mean. That is a speed-up of 4.49×, or 4.08× using measured points only, without interpolation. By target it is 4.89× at one epoch, 4.53× at two and 4.09× at three. o-means beat its paired e-means run for every seed, dataset and target: 114 of 114.

Against Lloyd’s k-means

We used two GPU implementations of Lloyd’s algorithm, both run for 25 full-batch iterations from the same random start:

  • Flash-KMeans 0.3.1, the reference used in the e-means paper.
  • faiss 1.14.3 GPU, with its own k-means loop, brute-force single-precision search and the data already on the GPU.

The two end within 0.02% of each other’s error. We then measured how long e-means and o-means take to reach the error of Flash-KMeans after its 25 iterations. This comparison was computed afterwards from runs that already existed. It was not among the acceptance criteria.

Distance computations. This count does not depend on anyone’s kernels. Lloyd spends 25 passes. e-means reaches the same error with a fit of 2 to 3 epochs. o-means reaches it at 1.6 to 3.2 epochs, each of which costs a fraction of a pass, for a total of 0.15 to 0.45 passes. On all eight datasets that is less arithmetic than half of one Lloyd iteration.

Wall-clock time. o-means reaches the same error 69–274 times sooner than Flash-KMeans (geometric mean 126×) and 78–337 times sooner than faiss GPU (geometric mean 181×). By the same measure, e-means is 27 times sooner than Flash-KMeans. Neither Lloyd library was tuned for this GPU; see Limitations.

Kernels versus algorithm

v0 runs e-means’ algorithm on our kernels and performs at least as many distance computations as e-means, so its gain over e-means is the kernel gain.

The algorithm’s share varies with k: 1.96× on SimpleWiki (k = 8,192) and 4.89× on SPACEV 10M (k = 65,536). With more centroids, a smaller fraction of them is searched.

On the five development datasets, where each change was added in turn, the cumulative speed-up over e-means was 1.51× with the kernels, 1.66× with the recency-weighted finish added, and 4.44× with two-level assignment added.

Distance computations and time. At the points where o-means matches e-means, it has done 0.06 to 0.30 of e-means’ distance computations, 0.13 in the geometric mean. About 7 times fewer distance computations buy about 3 times less time. The gap has three causes: the two-level kernel gathers scattered rows, cells are uneven in size, and the index has to be built and refreshed. On DEEP 1M that maintenance is a quarter of a one-epoch fit.

06

Limitations

  • One GPU. All timings are from one RTX 5090, with e-means on its Triton backend and not the H100 kernels it was tuned on. On an H100 the kernel share of the gain should shrink or disappear. The algorithm’s share has not been measured there, and the ratio between saved distance computations and saved time may differ on other hardware.
  • Lloyd baselines are not tuned for this GPU. Flash-KMeans’ distance kernel runs at 25–103 TFLOPS on the 5090, where we measured about 230 TFLOPS with cuBLAS in half precision. Its 25 iterations take about 4 to 16 times longer here than the e-means paper reports on an H100. faiss was run with its default single-precision search (26–52 TFLOPS here); we did not try its half-precision option. Part of the 100× factor reflects this, and the comparison was not pre-registered.
  • Approximate search during training. On typical data 0.5–2.4% of points miss their nearest centroid. Reported errors use exact assignment, and assign returns exact labels.
  • Probe counts were chosen on a few datasets. Cosine-like data such as GloVe-200 misses more often (4–13%) and loses about 0.1% in error. A larger nprobe narrows the gap at some cost in time.
  • Smaller gains at small k. With 8,192 centroids there are only 64 cells, and searching 12 of them covers about a fifth of the table. The three datasets we ran at this k (SimpleWiki, plus CC-News and CelebA outside the evaluation set) had the lowest speed-ups, 3.09× to 3.65×.
  • Not tested above 65,536 centroids. The evaluation covers up to 10 million vectors and 65,536 centroids. Larger indexes are a target use, such as the 262,144 clusters the faiss guidelines suggest for ten to a hundred million vectors. The gain grew with k inside the tested range, but we have not measured beyond it.
  • Some synthetic shapes. On synthetic, well-separated mixtures at 1,024 dimensions and above, a few cells can hold most of the centroids and the speed-up fades, with quality unaffected. This did not happen on any real dataset we ran.
  • Long runs. We tested up to 12.8 epochs. On four of the eight datasets, e-means at eight epochs ends 0.02–0.16% lower than anything o-means reaches in that range, at 2.7 to 3.2 times the time.
  • Scale of the evaluation. Three to five seeds per dataset, where the e-means paper uses ten. Speed-ups are read from seed-averaged curves with interpolation (4.08× without it). GPU only, Euclidean distance only.
07

Using o-means

o-means is in our fork of faiss as a contrib package, next to e-means, under faiss’s MIT licence, with tests and a benchmark script.

import torch
from faiss.contrib.o_means import Omeans

# x: float32 CUDA tensor of shape [n, d]
om = Omeans(d, k, nepochs=2.5)
centroids, labels = om.train(x)      # starts from k random rows of x
distances, nearest = om.assign(x)    # exact search
  • nepochs is the number of passes over the data, in the same unit as e-means’ epochs, and may be fractional. In our runs o-means matched 25 Lloyd iterations at 1.6 to 3.2 epochs. We tested values from 0.8 to 12.8.
  • nlist and nprobe override the number of cells and the number of cells each point searches. The defaults follow the rules in At a glance.
  • Labels. The labels returned by train come from the approximate search and were taken while the centroids were still moving. Call assign when the labels matter.
  • Memory. Peak GPU memory in our test runs was 7.5 GB, data included.

To compare o-means and e-means on one dataset:

python benchs/o_means/bench_o_means.py --nepochs 1 2 3

The paper has the full method and the evaluation protocol.
