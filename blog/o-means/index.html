o-means: k-means for large k in a quarter of e-means’ time

The Owlgebra team · November 2026

Code Release report Evaluation protocol Data behind the figures

o-means is a GPU k-means for large k: millions of vectors and tens of thousands of centroids, the job you run to build an IVF index or a codebook. It starts from e-means, the mini-batch method Meta FAIR published earlier this month, and replaces its most expensive step. Instead of comparing each point with every centroid, o-means searches a two-level structure and does between 6 and 22 percent of that work, depending on k and the dimension.

On eight datasets that we kept aside until the method was frozen, on one RTX 5090, o-means reached e-means’ clustering error 4.49 times sooner. That is a geometric mean over datasets and budgets; the worst case was 2.54 and the best 7.25. Two rows from the table further down: on GooAQ (1.46 million vectors, 768 dimensions, 16,384 clusters) e-means needs 0.50 s for two epochs and o-means reaches the same held-out error in 0.11 s. On SPACEV with 10 million vectors and 65,536 clusters it is 2.24 s against 0.36 s.

Against classic k-means most of the distance had already been covered by e-means. Twenty-five Lloyd iterations cost 25 full passes of distance computations. e-means matches their error in 2 to 3 passes. o-means matches it with the arithmetic of 0.15 to 0.45 passes.

The fine print belongs up here too. Every timing comes from a single RTX 5090. e-means’ fastest kernels need an H100, so on our card it ran its Triton backend; about 1.4 to 1.5× of our gain is faster kernels and about 3× is the algorithm. The assignment inside o-means is approximate. The rule for how many cells each point searches was fitted on a handful of datasets. There is no CPU version. Each number is a mean over 3 to 5 seeds. A later section lists where we would not trust the result.

01

Where k-means spends its time at large k

k-means looks for k centroids that minimise the mean squared distance from each of n points to its nearest centroid. Lloyd’s algorithm alternates two steps: assign every point to its nearest centroid, then move every centroid to the mean of its points. The assignment is the expensive half. One pass costs n·k distance computations in d dimensions, and at k = 16,384 or 65,536 nothing else in the loop comes close. We profiled e-means on our card: 80 to 86 percent of its wall time is the kernel that computes distances and takes the minimum.

There are two ways to make that cheaper. You can make fewer passes, or you can do less work per pass. e-means did the first. o-means keeps that and adds the second.

02

What e-means does

e-means is mini-batch k-means with the update rule of a VQ-VAE codebook. Each centroid is the ratio of two exponential moving averages, a running sum of the points assigned to it and a running count. After each batch both averages move a step toward the batch’s statistics, and the step size follows a cosine schedule from large to small. Clusters that stop receiving points are revived by splitting a busy centroid in two. Batch size and peak step are not tuned by the user; they come from two power laws in n, k and the number of epochs T that the authors fitted once. T is the only knob. At the end the centroids are replaced by the plain means of the last epoch’s points, under the labels those points received as they went by.

The e-means paper reports that this reaches the error of 25 Lloyd iterations in one to three epochs, which on an H100 is 10 to 45 times sooner than Flash-KMeans. It is a good algorithm and o-means keeps nearly all of it: the objective, the random starting centroids, the moving-average update, the two laws, the cosine schedule and the revival rule are unchanged.

What e-means leaves alone is the cost of an epoch. Every batch is still compared with all k centroids.

03

What o-means changes

Two-level assignment

Group the k centroids into cells of about 128. There are G = k/128 cells, rounded up to a multiple of 16: 128 cells for k = 16,384 and 512 for k = 65,536. The grouping is itself a small k-means, run on the centroids instead of the data. Then, for each point in a batch:

  1. compute its distance to the G cell centres and keep the p nearest cells;
  2. compute its distance to the centroids inside those p cells, and to no others;
  3. take the minimum.

p is 16 below 512 dimensions, 12 from 512 up, and 24 when k is above 16,384.

For k = 16,384 and p = 16 that is 128 + 16 × 128 = 2,176 distance computations per point instead of 16,384, about 13 percent. For k = 65,536 it is 512 + 24 × 128 = 3,584, about 5.5 percent. The measured counts, which include the cost of building the cells, agree: 13.4 percent on DEEP 1M and 6.0 percent on SPACEV 10M for a one-epoch fit.

On the GPU this is three kernel launches per batch. The first scores a block of points against all cell centres and drops each (point, cell) pair into that cell’s bucket. The second walks the buckets. We keep the centroid table physically sorted by cell, so each cell is one contiguous block and a tile of points can be multiplied against it on tensor cores like a small dense problem. The third takes the minimum over each point’s p partial results. Nothing is read back to the host inside the loop.

Cells go stale, and revival makes it worse

The cells describe where the centroids were when the cells were built. Centroids move after every batch, and e-means’ revival moves some of them a long way: during the first epoch 9 to 12 thousand of 16,384 centroids are split, and a freshly split centroid still sits in its donor’s cell on paper while it drifts off in practice.

Our first schedule rebuilt the cells twice per epoch. Between one eighth and one half of the first epoch, 10 to 28 percent of points were then assigned to something other than their nearest centroid, and the error after one epoch rose by 0.53 percent on DEEP 1M and 0.76 percent on arXiv-768. Crude early centroids were not the problem. Revival was.

Two changes fixed it. During the first epoch the cells are refreshed after every revival check, which is every five batches; the refresh reassigns centroids to the existing cell centres and re-sorts the table, with no k-means iterations, so it is cheap. After the first epoch the cells are rebuilt every half epoch. And every split is recorded as a pointer from donor to child, so a point that lands on a donor also checks the donor’s child and grandchild before it decides.

With both in place, 0.5 to 2.4 percent of points missed their nearest centroid on four of our five development datasets. GloVe-200 was the exception at 4 to 13 percent, which cost it about 0.1 percent in error. After the same number of epochs an o-means fit ends 0.02 to 0.13 percent higher in training error than the same fit with exact assignment, and each epoch takes between a quarter and a half as long (one seed, four development datasets).

A different closing mean for short runs

e-means closes by averaging the last epoch’s points under labels that are up to an epoch old. In a one-epoch run those labels include everything from the chaotic start. Below two epochs o-means weights each batch by its recency, with a time constant of one epoch, and when a centroid is split it hands half of the accumulated sums to the child. At one epoch this lowers validation error by 0.34 to 0.70 percent on the five development datasets, enough that o-means passes e-means’ one-epoch error before it has seen all the data once. From two epochs up the same trick raises training error (by 0.24 to 0.32 percent at two epochs), so there o-means goes back to e-means’ closing mean.

Kernels

Before touching the algorithm we rewrote e-means’ inner loop for our card: a fused half-precision kernel for distances and minimum, a fused update, and a revival step that does not wait on the host. We call e-means’ unchanged algorithm on these kernels v0. It matters for the accounting below.

04

How we measured

We reused the e-means paper’s benchmark rules wherever they are published and wrote ours down before running anything. The rules that decide what a number means:

Both methods start from the same k random rows of the data. The data is on the GPU before the clock starts. The clock covers everything the method does: casting, building its state, every pass, the closing step. Each fit gets an untimed warm-up call first, and fits shorter than two seconds are timed three times and the median kept. e-means and o-means fits are interleaved in one process, so drift in the machine hits both. e-means is called the way its own benchmark calls it, in bfloat16 with the batch law the paper prescribes for each size.

Error is the mean squared distance from held-out rows to their nearest centroid, computed with exact assignment. The approximation inside o-means is never used for scoring.

A method that takes an effort knob produces a curve of error against time, so comparing two of them needs a rule. Ours: run e-means at T = 1, 2, 3 and 5 epochs and o-means at ten efforts from 0.8 to 12.8 epochs, average over seeds, and for each T find the earliest time at which o-means’ curve is at or below e-means’ error. Between two neighbouring o-means points we interpolate in log-time, but only if their times differ by at most 1.6×. The speed-up at T is e-means’ time divided by that crossing time, and the headline score is the geometric mean over datasets and T = 1, 2, 3. We also report the strict version, in which only measured points count.

We developed on nine datasets and locked eight others away: SPACEV at 1M and 10M, ImageNet-CLIP, and five text and image embedding sets from the VIBE benchmark with 768 to 3,072 dimensions. The success criteria were fixed before the lock: score at least 1.15, no dataset-and-budget cell below 0.95, and error at e-means’ five-epoch time no more than 0.16 percent worse, which is the noise floor we measured for that comparison. The test suite was run once, on the release commit, and never again.

For scale, when we ran a copy of e-means with a different internal seed against e-means through the same harness it scored within 0.013 of 1.0.

The score is 4.49, or 4.08 if we refuse to interpolate between measured points. By target it is 4.89 at one epoch, 4.53 at two and 4.09 at three. The seven datasets with at most 1.5 million vectors have a geometric mean of 4.26; SPACEV 10M, the only test set with 65,536 centroids, has 6.55. All 38 runs finished, none produced a non-finite centroid, and peak GPU memory was 7.5 GB. Every seed on every dataset beat its own paired e-means run at all three targets, 114 of 114.

The development suites, which we did tune on, scored 4.44 (five datasets) and 4.61 (nine). The held-out number sits between them.

At equal time the two are close. At the moment e-means finishes five epochs, o-means’ error is lower by 0.12 to 0.49 percent. Our noise floor for that comparison is 0.16 percent, so on SPACEV 1M and ImageNet-CLIP “lower” is not established and “not worse” is the claim. The gain is in time.

The gain is smallest where k is small. Three of our seventeen datasets have k = 8,192: SimpleWiki in the test suite, CC-News and CelebA in development. They are the three lowest per-dataset speed-ups, 3.09 to 3.65, and SimpleWiki holds the weakest test cell at 2.54. With 8,192 centroids there are only 64 cells, and searching 12 of them covers about a fifth of the table.

06

Results against Lloyd’s k-means

The e-means paper’s reference for classic k-means on a GPU is Flash-KMeans run for 25 full-batch iterations from the same random start, and we kept it. When the benchmark was built we recorded that run’s held-out error and time for every dataset and seed. Here we ask how long e-means and o-means take to reach the same error. This comparison was not among the pre-registered criteria; we computed it afterwards from runs that already existed.

Count distance computations first, because that number does not depend on anyone’s kernels. Lloyd spends 25 passes’ worth. e-means reaches the same error with a run of 2 to 3 epochs. o-means reaches it at an effort of 1.6 to 3.2 epochs, each of which costs a fraction of a pass, for a total of 0.15 to 0.45 passes’ worth. On all eight test datasets that is less arithmetic than half of one Lloyd iteration.

Wall-clock needs more care. On our card Flash-KMeans takes 1.35 to 51.6 seconds for its 25 iterations and o-means reaches the same error in 0.020 to 0.37 seconds. That is 69 to 274 times sooner, geometric mean 126; by the same measure e-means is 27 times sooner than Flash-KMeans. We would not put those multiples in a headline. Flash-KMeans’ distance kernel runs at 24 to 103 TFLOPS on the 5090, where we measured about 230 with cuBLAS in half precision, and its 25 iterations take about 4 to 16 times longer here than the e-means paper reports for the same datasets on an H100. Part of the multiple is a baseline that is not at home on this GPU.

So we also timed the k-means most people actually run: faiss on the GPU, 25 iterations in single precision from the same start, with the data already on the card. It takes [[faiss_s_min]] to [[faiss_s_max]] seconds and ends within [[faiss_vs_flash_mse_max]] percent of Flash-KMeans’ error. o-means reaches faiss’s error [[x_faiss_min]] to [[x_faiss_max]] times sooner, geometric mean [[x_faiss_geomean]]. Called the usual way, with the data in host memory, the same 25 iterations take [[faiss_stock_overhead]] times as long.

Quality is not where these methods differ much. Run to the end of its ladder, 12.8 epochs, o-means ends 0.73 to 1.65 percent below Lloyd’s error, in 15 to 44 times less time than Flash-KMeans’ 25 iterations. About one percent of MSE is the size of the prize. What changes is how long it takes to collect.

07

Kernels or algorithm

e-means was built and tuned on H100s, where its fastest distance kernels run. They do not run on an RTX 5090, so the reference in this post used e-means’ Triton backend, and some of our speed-up is simply better kernels for this card. To separate the two we measured v0, which is e-means’ algorithm, unchanged, on our kernels. It performs as many distance computations as e-means, or more. Whatever it gains is kernels.

On the test suite that is 1.43 from kernels times 3.13 from the algorithm. The split varies by dataset. The algorithm’s share is 1.96 on SimpleWiki and 4.89 on SPACEV 10M: with more centroids, a smaller fraction of them is searched.

If you run e-means on an H100, expect the kernel share to shrink or disappear. We could not test that. The algorithm’s share has some support that does not depend on the card: at the points where o-means matches e-means it has done between 0.06 and 0.30 of e-means’ distance computations, 0.13 in the geometric mean. But look at the exchange rate. About 7 times fewer distance computations bought about 3 times less time. The two-level kernel gathers scattered rows, works on cells of uneven size, and pays for rebuilding the cells, which is a quarter of a one-epoch fit on DEEP 1M. On a GPU whose dense kernel is faster relative to its memory traffic, the rate could be worse. We do not know.

08

Things that did not work

Smaller batches. A simulation said that quartering the batch would cut error by 0.56 percent at two epochs. On real data the change was between −0.04 and +0.16 percent, for 3 to 18 percent more time. We had written down in advance what result would kill the idea, and it never got a scored run.

Exact re-assignment at the end. Re-assigning the oldest quarter of the last epoch against the final centroids lowers error by 0.23 to 0.43 percent at 1.25 epochs. It also adds 19 to 20 percent to the fit, and at the budgets that matter the same time spent on more mini-batches buys more. The score fell 3.8 percent and we reverted it.

Our first effort ladder. The first version of the new closing mean improved the score by 5.9 percent and still failed, because its ladder had a 2× hole between 3.2 and 6.4 epochs and one dataset’s three-epoch target fell into it. The benchmark gives no credit across a gap that wide. Fixing the ladder, with the algorithm untouched, turned 5.9 into 10.2 percent. Where you place the budgets you test is worth about 4 percent of the score. That is a property of the benchmark, and readers should discount accordingly.

An adaptive probe count. A variant that measures the miss rate during the fit and raises p when needed exists in the code. On GloVe-200 it recovers 0.04 to 0.06 percent of error for 55 to 64 percent more time, and on datasets that did not need it it costs 2 to 4 percent. It is not the default.

09

Where not to trust this

One GPU. All timings are from one RTX 5090 with e-means on its Triton backend. We have no H100 numbers. The kernel share of the gain would likely shrink there, and the algorithm share is unmeasured.

Approximate assignment. The centroids are trained with labels that are wrong for a small share of points. We score with exact assignment on held-out rows, so the reported errors are real, but there is no worst-case guarantee, and o-means returns centroids, not labels. If you need labels, run one exact assignment at the end.

A rule fitted on few datasets. The probe counts (16, 12, 24) are the smallest that kept misses near 1 percent on a handful of development datasets. GloVe-200, which is cosine-like data, misses 4 to 13 percent at p = 16. A dataset that behaves worse than GloVe will lose more than GloVe’s 0.1 percent unless you raise p or use the adaptive variant. Synthetic data can be harsher: on a mixture of 65,536 Gaussian modes in 96 dimensions with k = 16,384, o-means’ error after the same number of epochs was 1.2 to 1.7 percent above e-means’, against 0.2 percent with every cell searched. At equal time it was still ahead.

High dimension and hub cells. On synthetic, well-separated mixtures at 1,024 dimensions and above, a few cells swallow most of the centroids and points end up scanning 45 to 94 percent of the table. Quality is unaffected, but on a synthetic 1M × 2,048 shape o-means was only 1.04 to 1.16 times faster than our dense version. We did not see hub cells on real data, and we have only two real datasets at 2,048 dimensions or more.

Long budgets. Our ladder stops at 12.8 epochs, which ends before e-means’ eight-epoch run does. On 4 of the eight test datasets e-means at eight epochs ends 0.02 to 0.16 percent below anything on o-means’ ladder, at 2.7 to 3.2 times the time. If you want the last tenth of a percent and have time to spare, we have not shown that o-means gets you there.

Seeds and interpolation. Five seeds per dataset, three at 10M; the e-means paper uses ten. Speed-ups are read off seed-averaged curves with interpolation; the strict score is 4.08. e-means’ own run time is not monotone in T on small datasets (on SimpleWiki three epochs run faster than two, because its batch law doubles the batch), which flatters one cell. Scored against the best e-means time at or below each target error, the test score is 4.46.

Scope. GPU only. Nothing above 10 million vectors or 65,536 centroids, while the e-means paper goes into the billions. Nothing at small k or low dimension. Euclidean distance only.

10

Using it

o-means ships as a contrib package in our faiss fork, next to e-means, and takes the same arguments.

import torch
from faiss.contrib.o_means import Omeans

# x: float32 CUDA tensor [n, d], already on the GPU
om = Omeans(d, k, nepochs=2.5)
centroids, labels = om.train(x)        # starts from k random rows
distances, nearest = om.assign(x)      # full search, for when you need labels

nepochs is the number of passes over the data, the same unit as e-means’ epochs, and it can be fractional. In our runs o-means matched 25 Lloyd iterations at between 1.6 and 3.2 epochs, and the ladder we tested runs from 0.8 to 12.8. nlist and nprobe override the number of cells and how many of them each point searches; the defaults are the rule described above. The labels that train returns come from the approximate search, taken while the centroids were still moving, so call assign when the labels matter. Peak GPU memory in our test runs, data included, was 7.5 GB.

The package includes a small script that runs o-means and e-means on one dataset and prints time and error:

python benchs/o_means/bench_o_means.py --nepochs 1 2 3

The release report, the evaluation protocol and the data behind every figure and table are published with this post.
